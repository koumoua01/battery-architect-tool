# Battery Architect — connection layer generator

A small tool that turns a cell layout into cut files for the metal sheets that
connect the cells: the **top** sheet and the **bottom** sheet.

It does this:

1. **Arrange** cells (staggered or square grid, any cell size), turn the whole
   layout in 90° steps, add or drop cells one at a time on any row, and pick the
   cells you do not want so they leave the pack together.
2. **Group** them into islands — one island = one connected piece of metal.
3. **Connect** them: the tool builds the metal body that joins every cell of an
   island, with weld pads and straps, so the piece is always one solid part.
   New connections are drawn in as you make them, so you can see the strap
   being laid down.
4. **Cut** that body either as a solid plate or down to the weld lines — a pad
   on every spot and one band along the lines between them, with the fields
   between the bands falling out as slots. The bands can run along the rows,
   the diagonals or the columns.
5. **Mark** every weld spot: a short slit cut through each pad, centred on the
   cell — the guide line the strips on the market carry, so a big plate is no
   longer blind when you come to weld it.
6. **Orient** the cells: click to turn one over, and its + moves to the other
   face.
7. **Look through** either sheet at the other one's plate — dashed, and where it
   really lies in the pack — to check both ends of every cell at once.
8. **Mark the pack ends** — put the negative and positive tabs on any island,
   on either sheet — then trace and animate the main current path from one to
   the other.
9. **Export** the two sheets as 1:1 SVG, DXF, and a printable assembly map.

No installation, no build step, no internet. Double-click `index.html`.

```
index.html      the app (open this)
geometry.js     shape generation, contour extraction, SVG/DXF writers
tests/          headless checks + a PNG previewer
out/            example exports generated by the tests
docs/           background: feasibility and architecture notes
```

## Workflow

1. **Cells** — pick the cell, the gap between cells, rows, and cells per row,
   then *Fill grid*. Staggered packing is the hex layout used in most packs.
   The two arrow buttons turn the whole layout 90° (**R** turns it clockwise).
   Cell positions, islands, numbering and both sheets turn together. The turn
   is part of the document, so *Fill grid* later regenerates the layout
   sideways rather than snapping it back.
2. **Tools** — three tools:
   - **Paint** joins cells to the highlighted island. Drag to paint a run,
     *Shift*+click for a whole row, right-click to erase.
   - **Remove** picks the cells to take out of the pack — space for the BMS, a
     bolt, a vent, a corner you have to trim. Click a cell to pick it (click it
     again to drop it, *Shift*-click to add), or drag a box across the bare
     sheet to sweep up a group; picked cells are ringed in red and the bar at
     the bottom counts them. **Del** or that bar's *Remove* then takes them all
     out at once, as a single undo step, from both faces; **Esc** drops the lot
     without touching anything. The click itself never deletes, so you can see
     what you are about to lose. The islands keep the rest. If the removed cells
     were surrounded, the metal closes around them and leaves holes one cell
     wide, so take the cells around them too when you need real clearance.
   - **Flip** turns a cell over: its + moves to the other face. That is how a
     series chain is built, and the polarity count on the right tracks it.
   - **Ends** — the **Current** panel below the tools sets the pack's negative
     and positive tabs. Pick *−* or *+*, then click a pad on the sheet you are
     looking at. The tab is drawn on that pad and belongs to the island it sits
     on; both ends may be on either sheet. The panel then traces the main path
     through the cells and animates it from *−* to *+*. **C** shows or hides the
     flow, and the assembly map carries the same ends and route.
3. **Rows** — the list has one line per row with its cell count. **−** and **+**
   take one cell off or add one to that row (hold *Shift* to work on the left
   end instead of the right); **+ row** and **− row** add or drop a whole row.
   Hovering a line highlights that row on the sheet. A cell added this way joins
   the nearest island on each face, so it is never left unwelded.
4. **Face** — switch between the TOP and BOTTOM sheet. You are always editing
   the same cells; the bottom view is the mirrored sheet, exactly as it will be
   cut. Tick **See the other sheet through** (or press **X**) to have the other
   plate drawn on the same view as well.
5. **Islands** — click a colour, then click cells to paint them into that
   island. *Auto stripe* lays out a complete starting design in one click.
6. **Geometry** — the numbers behind the metal, one slider each: *Pad D*,
   *Strap W*, *Kerf*, *Margin*, *Precision*, plus *Guide L* once the guide slits
   are on and *Angle* once you are cutting to the weld lines or marking them.
   The sheets follow the thumb while it is still down, so a shape is found by
   eye rather than by trial and error, and one whole drag is a single **Ctrl+Z**.
   Raise *Strap W* towards the cell pitch to get a solid plate; lower it for
   narrow nickel straps. A number that has left its default turns blue, its row
   grows a **↺** that puts that one back, and **reset** in the section header
   puts them all back at once — a double-click on a slider does the same as its
   ↺.

   The **Plate / Weld lines** switch at the top of the section decides what the
   metal is: *Plate* is the union of every pad and strap — one solid piece per
   island. *Weld lines* cuts that same island down to what the welder and the
   current actually need, a pad on every spot and one band along the lines
   between them; the fields between the bands are cut away, so the piece becomes
   a routed strip with slots in it. Switching is one undo step, and it changes
   what the ↺ arrows call a default: a plate wants an 18 mm pad, a routed strip
   wants 9.5 and a 6.5 mm band. The first time you switch, if the widths are
   still plate-sized, both are brought down to the strip's defaults — otherwise
   the bands would meet and there would be nothing to cut away.

   *Angle* turns the route and only offers directions the packing has: 0° runs
   the bands along the rows, 90° across them, and 30°/60° along the diagonals of
   a staggered pack (45° on a square one). On a staggered pack 45° is not one of
   them — the route would have to cross itself and the slots would fill in — so
   the slider does not stop there.

   **Cell guide slits**, the switch under the cut styles, cuts one short slit
   through every weld pad, at the cell centre — the small line a bought-in strip
   carries. It works on either cut style: a plate with a guide line in every
   pad, or a routed strip with a slit in every spot. *Angle* turns the slits
   too, so they can follow the metal instead of crossing it, and the slit is
   always kept inside its pad.
7. **Export** — SVG or DXF, both sheets or one, plus the assembly map.

**Ctrl+Z** undoes anything here, including a whole picked group in one step.

Your work is kept in browser storage and survives a reload. *Save .json* writes
a file you can keep or share; *Load .json* reads it back.

## Speed

The shape has to be rebuilt whenever a cell, an island or a pad size changes,
and on a 98-cell pack that used to take about a second — long enough that
deleting a cell felt like a stall. It is now about 30 ms for both sheets, and
about 5 ms for the usual edit:

| | before | now |
|---|---|---|
| one sheet, 98 cells, 7 islands | 490 ms | 18 ms |
| both sheets | 1035 ms | 35 ms |
| both sheets cut to the weld lines | — | 30 ms |
| both sheets with a guide slit in every pad | — | 35 ms |
| one island | 79 ms | 2.7 ms |
| delete a cell (rebuilds both faces) | ~1000 ms | ~5 ms |

The weld-line cut is the cheaper of the two shapes because each island is one
route — a pad per cell and a band per step — where the plate also draws every
strap between near neighbours, about three per cell.

Three things did it, in order of how much they mattered:

- **The contour grid is no longer tied to the drawing tolerance.** The boundary
  is a smooth arc, so linear interpolation across a grid edge lands a quarter of
  a thousandth of a millimetre from the true arc even at a coarse step. What is
  left is the chord error of the output polyline, which is the simplification
  tolerance — that is what the *Precision* slider controls. A 3.4× coarser grid
  costs nothing measurable in accuracy and divides the sampling work by 12.
- **A bucketed distance field.** Each pad and strap is filed under the grid
  buckets it touches, grown by one grid step, and a sample looks only at its
  own bucket. Growing by exactly one step is the right amount: of the two
  samples that straddle a crossing, one is inside and the other is within a
  step of the boundary, so every value an interpolation can use is exact.
  Pads and straps are kept apart so the minimum is taken over squared distances
  and square-rooted twice per sample instead of once per primitive.
- **Per-island contour cache.** Painting one cell changes one island, but a
  rebuild used to re-contour all fourteen. The cache key is the island's
  geometry written out in full — cell ids and their exact positions on that
  face — so any edit that moves or replaces a cell misses the cache without
  anyone having to remember to invalidate it. Nothing can go stale.

`node tests/bench.js` reproduces the table. The browser smoke test times a real
delete click end to end — geometry, panels, animation set-up — and prints it
next to the result; it reads about 8 ms.

Picking costs nothing: a click only marks a cell, so sweeping a box over forty
cells is forty redraws and one rebuild — the shape is built once, when you
confirm.

A slider drag is the same trick. The face on screen is rebuilt under the thumb
— every 55 ms on a 98-cell pack, and spaced out further on a big one according
to what the last preview cost — and only that face, so it is half the work.
Both faces are then built once, exactly, when the thumb stops. Nothing is
half-built by the time you let go. The see-through layer costs nothing here: the
ghost is not re-traced on every preview, it keeps the last settled shape and is
rebuilt with the rest on release (and dropped outright if the change moved the
sheet, since it is drawn in those coordinates).

## How an island becomes a shape

For every cell in the island the tool places a **weld pad**: a disc of
diameter *Pad D*, centred on the terminal. Every pair of cells that are near
neighbours is joined by a **strap**: a capsule of width *Strap W*. The island is
the union of those shapes.

The union is contoured numerically — a signed distance field sampled on a grid
(0.34 mm, finer only if a strap is thinner than that), traced with marching
squares, then simplified. That gives one clean closed polyline per piece, plus
holes if the layout has any. Kerf is applied before tracing by growing every
pad and strap by `kerf / 2`, which is exactly an outward offset of the finished
piece. Nothing is stored pre-kerfed; change the kerf and the cut path changes.

The *Precision* slider sets the simplification tolerance, not the sampling
step: 0.1 mm means the cut path stays within 0.034 mm of the true outline,
which is four times finer than the marker you would use to check it.

Near neighbours means "close to the typical cell spacing", not a minimum
spanning tree. A tree is minimal but produces long single-strand necks and, on a
hex lattice, the in-row and diagonal distances are identical, so the tie-break
decides the shape. Connecting all near neighbours fills the gaps between rows.

### Cutting to the weld lines

The **Weld lines** cut uses the same pads and the same contourer, but joins them
with one route instead of a mesh: the island's cells are sorted into bands
across the direction of the metal, each band is walked end to end, and the next
band is entered at the end nearest the strip's free end. That is a
boustrophedon — it never doubles back over itself, it visits every cell exactly
once, and on a staggered pack it produces the zigzag of a bought-in slotted
strip. The bands are the rows at 0°, the two diagonals at 30° and 60°, and the
columns at 90°; that is why the angle slider has those stops and not others.

A path rather than a mesh of thin straps, because a narrow mesh is what lowering
*Strap W* already gives you, and it looks like a plate with small holes. A route
keeps continuous metal with one clear current path and slots wide enough to
flex, which is what the slotted strips are for: the sheet can sit flat on cells
that are not all exactly the same height, and it uses a third of the nickel. The
trade is electrical — the cells on one strip are still in parallel, but the
metal between them is now a chain, so a cell at the far end of the route reaches
the tab through every band before it and the strip shares current less evenly
than a plate does. Keep the route short and the band wide when that matters.

### Guide slits

A plate is blind. The cells are underneath it and the welder has to guess where
each terminal sits — and on a plate the guess is the whole job, because the
electrode has to land on the cell and not on the gap beside it.

**Cell guide slits** cut the mark that strips on the market carry: one short
slit through every weld pad, centred on the cell, 1.2 mm wide and as long as
*Guide L* says. It is a real cut, not an engraving — the plate comes off the
cutter with a slot in every pad, and the slot is what you line the electrode up
on: you can see it, and feel it under a finger when the light is bad.

The length is bounded at both ends. The slit has to stay inside the pad, and it
has to leave a rim at each end, because that rim is the only thing joining the
two halves of the pad — so the slider stops at 60 % of the pad and starts at
45 % of it, which gives an 18 mm plate pad an 8 mm slit and a 9.5 mm strip pad a
4.5 mm one. A slit that no longer fits is brought down with the pad rather than
left at a length the metal does not have room for.

The slits come out of the metal area, they are in the document, in the exports
and on the assembly map, and turning them on costs no tracing at all: the plate
is the one that was already built, so the switch is instant. *Angle* sets their
direction, and it is the same angle the bands use — keep the two together on a
routed strip, so the slit runs along the current's path instead of making it
squeeze round the end of the slot.

They are a guide, not a feature of the circuit. A slit removes metal from the
pad it marks, so it is one short line per spot, straight, and never longer than
the pad: if you need a slot that carries current or one that lets the sheet
flex, that is the weld-line cut, not this.

## The two sheets

The bottom sheet is the **exact reflection** of the top sheet about the sheet's
vertical centre line. You cut it flat and flip it over to fit, so reflection is
right and a plain re-draw is not. Because both sheets share one cell list, the
two can never disagree about where the cells are — which is the failure mode in
a hand-drawn pair. Rotating the pack does not change this rule: the bottom cut
file is still the mirror of the pack position, whatever way the pack is turned.

Polarity signs are computed, never stored twice: a cell is either flipped or
not, and the sign shown on each face follows from that. The **Flip** tool
toggles the cell you click (**F** does the same under the cursor). *Auto stripe*
flips alternate bands so every island on the top face has a single terminal
sign, which makes the bottom islands series junctions.

### Shorts and current paths

The checker reads the pack as a netlist. Each island is a conductor, and each
cell is a voltage source between the island on its + face and the island on its
− face, so a cell is a directed edge from one island to the other. Give the
islands potentials and every cell adds exactly one step: a series chain is a
straight path, and a parallel group is several edges taking the same step. If a
loop comes back with a different number of steps than it left with, the loop is
a short, because cells are wired back into themselves through metal of the wrong
polarity. This catches the obvious case — one cell turned over inside a parallel
group, so it shares both islands with its neighbours — and longer loops across
several islands. It deliberately accepts a mixed-polarity island: on the bottom
face that is the series junction, and the potential check tells the two apart.

Current also has to have somewhere to go. Each island's metal graph is the same
graph the shape is built from (every near-neighbour strap on a plate, the
boustrophedon route on a weld-line strip). The checker reports an island whose
graph has more than one component as an open current path, and a link much
longer than one pitch as a weak one. On a plate it also counts single-strap
necks — places where one strap is the only path — and reports them as notes. A
weld-line strip is a chain by design, so a very long one is noted instead:
current from the far cell passes through every band before it reaches the tab,
which is why short strips share current more evenly.

### Pack ends and the main current path

The **Current** panel answers the other half of the question: where does the
pack actually deliver its power? Pick *−* or *+*, then click a pad. The end is
a tab welded at that pad, but electrically it belongs to the **whole island** —
which is why both ends can sit on the bottom sheet, or one on each, exactly as
the layout needs. The button keeps the sheet and cell number, and the sheet
shows a coloured ring plus a `PACK −` / `PACK +` tag. A ring on the sheet you
are not looking at is dashed, so you can still see the far end.

Once both ends exist, the tool follows the same directed cell edges the short
checker uses: from the negative end's island, through a cell to its + island,
through that island's metal to the next cell's − pad, and so on until it reaches
the positive end. The metal part of every hop is the actual strap graph used to
draw the piece, so the red route follows the straps on a plate and the routed
bands on a weld-line strip instead of cutting across a slot. The route is drawn
as moving red dashes with arrowheads; the dashes run from *−* to *+*, and **C**
turns the overlay off and on. With reduced motion asked for, the route is drawn
once with static dashes.

If the cells between the ends do not form a directed chain — a reversed group,
a gap in the islands, or both ends on the same island — the panel says so and
the Checks list warns instead of drawing a line that does not exist. The trace
is a representative series path, not a full circuit solve: it does not split
current between parallel cells or model resistance, so it shows the route and
the direction, not the current in each strap.

### Seeing the other sheet through

The one thing you cannot see while working on a sheet is the other one: which
cells the far strip already holds, whether the two sheets alternate or land on
top of each other, and whether the far plate reaches a cell you have just left
out of this one. Tick **See the other sheet through** under the face buttons
(or press **X**) and the other plate is drawn on the same view too — dashed,
and underneath this one, so the sheet you are editing stays the subject.

It is drawn where that metal lies in the pack, not as it prints. The far sheet
is built in print coordinates — mirrored in x — so the drawing undoes the
mirror: a far pad appears on the cell it welds, not on the cell it would be cut
over. That is the difference between an overlay you can read and a mirrored one
that quietly lies to you. Each far piece keeps its own colour and its own dash,
so the two faces are told apart by the dash rather than by a second palette.

The ghost is a view option: it is not in the document, not in the exports and
not in the undo stack, and it costs nothing while it is off. You can only edit
the face you are looking at, so the ghost is a reference, never a target —
painting, picking and the row buttons all still work on the near sheet. While a
slider is being dragged the ghost holds its last build and is rebuilt with
everything else when the thumb stops.

## The animation

An edit is drawn, not just swapped. When you paint a cell, the straps that reach
it are laid down one after another, starting at the cell you touched, in about a
quarter of a second; the pad fades in as a ring. When a cell is removed, its
straps thin out and fade and its circle shrinks away. Both are found by diffing
the island's primitive list before and after the edit — the same list the
geometry is built from — so a strap that merely shifted by a hundredth of a
millimetre is not animated, and a connection that really did appear is. A drag
over thirty cells animates as one motion, because the primitives are
photographed once when the pointer goes down and diffed once when it comes up.

Frame pacing is honest: the canvas only redraws while something is moving, and
`prefers-reduced-motion` turns the whole thing off in favour of the final
picture. The current-flow overlay follows the same rule: it asks for frames only
while both ends trace a path, is throttled to about 30 fps, and becomes a static
dashed route when motion is reduced.

## Cutting notes

- **Kerf** is the width your process removes. Laser: measure it on a test piece,
  typically 0.1–0.3 mm depending on material and thickness. A craft knife or
  scissors: leave it at 0 and trim.
- **Island gap.** Two islands on the same face sit one cell row apart, so the
  metal between them is thin. The checker warns when they are close enough to
  cut into each other or to leave a web too thin to survive.
- **The weld-line strip** only has slots if the band is narrower than the gap
  between bands — about half the pitch. Push *Strap W* back up towards the pitch
  and the bands meet, the slots close, and you get a plate with a strange
  outline; the note under the sliders is the only warning you get. Every strip
  is also a chain, so check the current path rather than assuming the metal is
  one bus.
- **Guide slits** are 1.2 mm wide: wide enough to see and to cut, narrow enough
  to leave even a 9.5 mm strip pad a solid rim at both ends. Keep *Guide L*
  well under the pad — the rim is what joins the two halves of the weld spot.
- **SVG** is in millimetres and prints 1:1 in any browser — set the print scale
  to 100% and turn off "fit to page". Good for a paper template and directly
  usable by most laser software.
- **DXF** is R12 with millimetres as units, one closed polyline per contour, on
  layers `TOP_CUT` and `BOTTOM_CUT`.
- **The assembly map** is a 1:1 SVG of both faces with cell numbers, polarity
  signs, island colours and a legend. Print it and keep it next to the pack.
- Cut a small test coupon first — four cells, one island — and check the pads
  sit where you expect on a real cell, the kerf is right, and the pieces do not
  fall apart in your hand.

## Checks

The panel on the right reports electrical and fabrication problems. The
**Electrical** row in *Sheet* says at a glance whether the pack is shorted, and
the **Ends** and **Path** rows say where the main tabs are and how many cells the
traced series route visits:

- **a short circuit** — two cells that share both islands but face opposite
  ways, or a longer loop across several islands whose cell voltages do not add
  up. Parallel cells pointing the same way are normal and are not reported; a
  flipped whole group is the series junction, also normal.
- a terminal with no island on that face (it would stay unwelded),
- a cell claimed by two islands on one face, listed twice in one island, or an
  island pointing at a cell that is not in the pack,
- an island whose cells are not joined by metal — separate current paths on
  one piece,
- two islands too close to each other, and an island that has come apart into
  two or more pieces,
- two cells whose cans overlap, a duplicated cell id, or a cell with no valid
  position,
- a pad wider than the cell, so the metal reaches past the can,
- a weld-line band as wide as the gap between bands, so the slots have closed,
- a single strap spanning much more than one cell pitch — a long, weak current
  path,
- a guide slit longer than the pad allows (it is cut at the shorter length),
- pack ends on the same pad or island, an end on a pad with no island, or no
  directed series path between the two ends.

Errors are red, warnings amber and notes grey. The notes are the current-flow
detail rather than faults: an island that is a single chain, a plate with a
single-strap neck where there is no alternate path, or the length of the main
path between the two pack ends. Errors and warnings still export, because
sometimes you know better.

`tests/run-tests.js` also writes a weld-line pair — `out/pack-lines.svg`,
`out/pack-lines.dxf`, `out/assembly-map-lines.svg` — so the routed strip can be
looked at next to the plate in `out/pack.svg`. It checks the route itself: every
cell visited once, every step shorter than two pitches, one pad per spot, one
piece per strip, no field left enclosed, and that each of the packing's
directions produces a clean strip. It builds the electrical netlist too: a
reversed cell inside a parallel group is a short, a whole flipped group is a
series junction, two clusters are an open current path, overlapping cans and a
pad wider than the cell are caught, and a weld-line strap as wide as the band
spacing closes the slots. The pack ends are tested from both sides: a chain that
starts and ends on the bottom sheet traces every series cell in order, the metal
route is carried, a missing cell or island is reported, and a broken direction
opens the path. A slitted pair is written beside it —
`out/pack-guides.svg` — and the slits are checked the same way: one per spot,
inside the pad, out of the metal area, mirrored with the bottom sheet, carried
by both writers, and free of a re-trace when they are switched on.

`tests/smoke-app.js` writes a copy of the app with a script that drives the
real buttons — turn, pick cells and remove them, undo, paint, the row buttons,
the flip tool, deleting a whole island — then reads the result out of the page.
Run it in a headless browser and look for the `PASS` / `FAIL` block:

```
node tests/smoke-app.js
node tests/run-smoke.js
```

`tests/run-smoke.js` starts its own headless Chrome and prints the block
directly. The `--dump-dom` route also works, but the page must have finished by
the time the DOM is dumped, or the `<pre>` with the results is not there yet:

```
chrome --headless=new --dump-dom file:///.../out/app-smoke.html > out/app-smoke.txt
```

`tests/drag-app.js` is the other browser harness, and it drives Chrome rather
than the page: through the DevTools protocol it presses on a slider thumb and
drags with real mouse input, then reads the sheet with the button still down.
Page script cannot move a native range thumb, and the defect these sliders had
was exactly of that kind — the number moved, the drawing did not until the
button came up — so it takes trusted input to keep it fixed. It starts its own
headless Chrome and prints `DRAG PASS` / `DRAG FAIL`.

`geometry.js` has no dependencies and runs in Node or the browser. Everything
is millimetres.

## Limits

- Cells are placed by the grid generator; there is no free-hand placement, and
  rotation is in 90° steps. Rows grow and shrink at their ends — you cannot
  insert a cell into the middle of a row and push the rest along.
- One strap width for the whole pack, one pad size. Real designs sometimes mix.
- The weld-line cut is one route per island, so a big island becomes a long
  snake. Striping the pack into two-row islands — *Auto stripe* does that — is
  what keeps the strips short, and there is no way yet to route a ribbon that
  branches or to route two strips and join them.
- Picking is a pending edit, not a saved selection: the ringed cells belong to
  the Remove tool, *Esc* drops them and so does switching tool.
- While a slider is being dragged only the face you are looking at is traced, so
  anything that reads both faces — exporting, the assembly map, a face switch —
  rebuilds them first. With the other sheet showing through, its ghost keeps the
  last settled shape until you let go of the thumb.
- The other sheet is drawn, not simulated: it shows where the far metal lies but
  not how the pack will close, flex or sit. Two plates that overlap on screen
  are on opposite ends of the cells and cannot touch.
- No plated-through holes, fuses or BMS wire tabs yet — the guide slits are the
  one deliberate cut inside a pad.
- The guide slits mark the spot; they are not a circuit feature. One straight
  slit per pad, always shorter than the pad, and no way to place a mark anywhere
  but the cell centre.
- Removing cells makes a plain gap, not a shaped cutout.
- The pack ends are annotations, not extra tabs in the cut file: they mark the
  pad and the island the main lead belongs to, and appear on screen and in the
  assembly map. The red route is one representative series path between those
  ends, not a current-sharing or resistance solve, so it does not show how much
  current each parallel cell carries.
- The animation is decoration: it never changes the geometry, and with
  reduced-motion turned on you get the same result without it.
- DRC is deliberately shallow: it checks the things that actually ruin a pack
  (unwelded terminal, double-claimed cell, redundant or missing series path,
  overlapping cans, islands cutting into each other, a piece that has come
  apart, a closed weld-line slot, a single-strap neck), not current capacity,
  fuse sizing or thermal behaviour. It assumes all cells are the same type and
  voltage, which is the one type the grid generator places.
